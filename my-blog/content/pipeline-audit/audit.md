# The 15-Minute Sales Pipeline Self-Audit

<p class="subtitle">Twenty yes/no questions, a score, and the fixes that matter most.</p>

</div>

## How to use this audit

Set a timer for fifteen minutes. Open your CRM, or whatever you use to track deals, even if that is a spreadsheet or your inbox.

1. **Answer the scorecard (pages 2–3).** Twenty yes/no questions in five sections. Answer for how things work *today*, not how they are supposed to work. If you have to think hard about it, the answer is no.
2. **Total your score (page 4)** and read what your band means.
3. **Compare against the benchmarks (page 5)** to find the stage where you lose the most deals.
4. **Read the leak that matches your weakest section (pages 6–7)** and apply its fix this week.
5. **Do the attribution test (page 8).** It takes three minutes and is the most revealing page in this guide.

### Who this is for

Founders doing sales themselves, or with a first sales hire, at roughly 5 to 200 deals a year. The questions assume B2B sales with a human conversation somewhere in the process. If you sell self-serve with no sales touch, the attribution and close-rate sections still apply; skip what doesn't fit.

### One rule

Be honest. Nobody sees your score. A low score is useful: it tells you exactly where the next dollar of revenue is hiding. A flattering score just hides the leak for another quarter.

## The scorecard, part 1

Score **1 point for each "yes"**. Answer for what is true today.

### A. Lead sources

| # | Question | Yes | No |
|---|---|:-:|:-:|
| 1 | Can you name your top three lead sources, in order, from data rather than gut feel? | ☐ | ☐ |
| 2 | Does every new lead in your CRM have a recorded source? | ☐ | ☐ |
| 3 | Do you know roughly what each lead source costs you, in money or time? | ☐ | ☐ |
| 4 | Has any single source produced more than one deal in the last 90 days? | ☐ | ☐ |

### B. Follow-up speed

| # | Question | Yes | No |
|---|---|:-:|:-:|
| 5 | Do inbound leads get a human reply within one business hour? | ☐ | ☐ |
| 6 | Is there a named owner for every open lead? | ☐ | ☐ |
| 7 | Do you follow up at least three times before marking a lead as dead? | ☐ | ☐ |
| 8 | Does every open deal have a scheduled next step with a date? | ☐ | ☐ |

<p class="note">Most pipelines lose more deals to slow or missing follow-up than to losing on price. If you answered "no" to two or more questions in section B, start there.</p>

### What your total means

**16–20: Healthy.** Your pipeline is instrumented and you can make decisions from it. Your gains now come from optimizing the best channel and tightening the stage with the biggest drop-off. Go to the benchmarks and find your one outlier.

**11–15: Leaking.** The basics exist but there are gaps that cost you deals every month, usually in follow-up or attribution. Pick your lowest section and fix that first. Fixing one section well beats fixing three halfway.

**6–10: Running on memory.** Deals live in your head and your inbox. That works up to a point, and you are probably past it. Start with stage definitions (section C): everything else depends on knowing where a deal actually is.

**0–5: Flying blind.** That is common at the very beginning, and it is fixable in a week. Start with the attribution test on page 8, then add a source and a next step to every open deal.

### Your weakest section

Any section where you scored **2 or less out of 4** is a leak. Find it in the leak list on pages 6–7. If you have more than one, start with whichever comes first in this order: **B → C → D → A → E**. Speed and stage hygiene come first because the other fixes depend on them.

## Benchmarks: what healthy looks like

These are **directional ranges for early-stage B2B sales**, drawn from common practice rather than a single study. Your market, deal size, and sales motion will move them. Use them to spot the stage that is clearly out of line, not as targets to hit.

| Stage | Healthy | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| **Lead → first human reply** | Same business hour | More than one business day |
| **Lead → qualified conversation** | 20–40% of inbound leads | Under 10%, or you don't know |
| **Qualified conversation → opportunity** | 40–60% | Under 25% |
| **Opportunity → proposal sent** | 50–70% | Proposals sent to almost everyone, or almost no one |
| **Proposal → closed-won** | 20–35% | Under 15%, or above 60% (you may be underpricing) |
| **Lost deals with a recorded reason** | 90%+ | Under half |
| **Closed deals with a known source** | 80%+ | Under half |
| **Open deals untouched for 30+ days** | Under 20% of pipeline | Over 40% |
| **Sales cycle trend** | Stable or shortening | Growing every quarter |

### How to read your benchmarks

Work out your conversion at each stage for the last 90 days. You need only five counts from your CRM: leads, qualified conversations, opportunities, proposals, and wins.

Then find the **single biggest drop relative to the healthy range**. That stage is your constraint. Improving any other stage just pushes more deals into the bottleneck.

A very high close rate is not always good news. If you win more than half of your proposals, you are either very selective about who gets one or you are leaving money on the table. Test a higher price on your next three deals.

## The five most common pipeline leaks

### Leak 1: Slow first response

A lead fills out your form on Tuesday morning and hears back on Thursday. By then they have booked calls with two competitors, and the urgency that made them reach out has faded. Inbound interest decays in hours, not days.

**The fix:** Set a one-business-hour rule for the first human reply, and make it someone's named job. Route new leads to a phone notification or a dedicated Slack channel rather than a shared inbox. Keep a short reply template that asks one qualifying question and offers two specific meeting times. Speed beats polish here: a fast, plain reply wins more meetings than a perfect one sent tomorrow.

### Leak 2: Deals without a next step

A good call ends with "let's stay in touch", and the deal quietly dies over the next three weeks. Nobody lost it; nobody moved it either. This is the single most common reason pipelines look full but don't close.

**The fix:** No deal leaves a meeting without a dated next step that the buyer agreed to, written into the CRM before you close the call notes. Once a week, filter for open deals with no future activity and either schedule something or close them as lost. A smaller, honest pipeline forecasts better and gets more of your attention.

### Leak 3: Stages that mean different things to different people

"Proposal" means a PDF was emailed to one rep and a pricing discussion to another. Your stage conversion numbers are then meaningless, and the forecast is a guess.

**The fix:** Rewrite each stage as a buyer action with a clear exit criterion, such as "Buyer confirmed budget range and decision date" instead of "Discovery done". Keep it to five or six stages. Put the definitions at the top of your CRM pipeline view so they are visible every time someone drags a deal.

<div class="page-break"></div>

### Leak 4: Not knowing which channel produces revenue

You know LinkedIn sends the most leads, but not that the podcast you sponsored sent the three deals that actually closed. So you keep funding the channel that fills the top of the funnel and cut the one that fills the bank account.

**The fix:** Record a source on every lead at creation, and carry it through to closed-won so you can report revenue by channel, not just leads by channel. Track both the first touch and the touch that converted, since they are often different. Review revenue by source monthly and move budget toward the channel with the best revenue per dollar or hour spent. Page 8 shows you how big this gap is for you.

### Leak 5: Losing deals without learning why

Lost deals get marked "closed-lost" with no reason, so the same objection kills the next five deals too. You can't fix a pattern you never write down.

**The fix:** Make "lost reason" a required field with five or six fixed options (price, timing, chose a competitor, no decision, bad fit, went silent) plus a free-text note. Once a quarter, sort lost deals by reason. If one reason is more than a third of losses, that is your next project, whether it means changing pricing, qualification, or the product itself.

### Where to start

Pick **one** leak, the one matching your weakest scorecard section, and give it two weeks before moving to the next. Each fix takes an afternoon to set up and a few minutes a day to keep up, and the payoff compounds because every later stage gets better input.

| Weakest section | Start with |
|---|---|
| A. Lead sources | Leak 4 |
| B. Follow-up speed | Leaks 1 and 2 |
| C. Stage definitions | Leak 3 |
| D. Attribution | Leak 4 |
| E. Close rates | Leak 5 |

## The attribution test

### Do you know which channel your last 10 deals came from?

**Count the ticks in the last column.**

- **8–10:** Your attribution is in good shape. Check whether revenue per channel matches where you spend.
- **4–7:** You are making channel decisions on partial data. The deals you can't trace are usually from the channels hardest to measure, such as word of mouth, podcasts, and social, which are often your best.
- **0–3:** You don't know what is working. Every marketing dollar is a guess.

### Questions to bring to your next pipeline review

- Which stage has the biggest drop compared with the benchmarks, and what is the one change that would move it?
- Which channel produced the most *revenue* last quarter, and is it getting the most budget?
- What was the most common lost reason, and what are we doing about it?
